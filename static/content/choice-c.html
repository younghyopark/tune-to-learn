Stiff controllers dominate teleop — but do they have to?

We surveyed the literature and found that most existing teleoperated datasets do not explicitly report their controller gain settings. By analyzing the response curves in their papers and code, we can infer that the community overwhelmingly defaults to stiff, heavily damped controllers — yet with no shared rationale or principled justification.

Compliant Gains Can Teleop Just as Well

A common justification for stiff controllers is that they make teleoperation feel more responsive and precise — the robot tracks the operator's commands tightly, giving a sense of direct control. But this reasoning conflates controller stiffness with operator experience. A critical factor is the input mapping $\phi(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{x}) \rightarrow \mathbf{x}_{\text{des}}$ that translates user commands into position targets — it substantially modulates how the robot feels to the operator. To ensure a fair comparison across gain settings, we allow each participant to calibrate $\phi$ for each gain configuration before evaluation, yielding the gain-specific optimum:

$\phi^\star(\mathbf{K}) = \arg\max_\phi \; \mathcal{Q}(\phi; \mathbf{K})$

where $\mathcal{Q}$ denotes the operator's perceived control quality. This lets operators compare each gain configuration at its best achievable teleop experience. In our study, participants teleoperated a Franka Research 3 using a 6-DoF SpaceMouse to command delta movements in SE(3) space. The mapping takes the form:

$\mathbf{x}_{\text{des}}(t) = \alpha\,\mathbf{u}(t) + \beta\,\mathbf{x}(t) + (1-\beta)\,\mathbf{x}_{\text{des}}(t-1)$

where $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}_0^+$ is a scaling factor and $\beta \in \{0, 1\}$ selects between the current robot pose or the previous target as the integration base. After calibration, participants performed a non-prehensile box pushing task with gain configurations randomly sampled and blindly presented. We recorded task completion time, success/failure, and subjective 1–5 control quality ratings across 500 trials. The results show that when each gain setting is evaluated with its own optimized input mapping, compliant and overdamped settings achieve comparable success rates, similar completion times, and high subjective ratings from operators. The key insight is that appropriately tuning the scaling factor $\alpha$ compensates for the reduced responsiveness of compliant gains — the teleop penalty most practitioners fear from softer gains largely disappears once the interface is properly calibrated, removing a major barrier to adopting gain settings that are far more favorable for downstream policy learning.
